## Missing rainfall data

Rainfall records often have **gaps**: the observer was absent, the gauge was damaged, a chart was lost, a telemetry link failed. Most analyses (frequency analysis, water balance, a continuous simulation) need a complete record, so the gap is filled by estimating the missing value from **neighbouring stations**.

- The station with the missing value is the **interpolation station** (here called $X$).
- The neighbouring stations used for the estimate are the **index stations**. Choose at least three, as close and evenly placed around $X$ as possible.
- The **normal rainfall** $N$ of a station is its average rainfall for a particular date, month, season or year over a specified **30-year period**. Normals are recomputed every decade (the current climatological standard normal period is 1991–2020).

## Method 1 — Arithmetic average

If the normal annual precipitation of **every** index station is within **±10 %** of the normal annual precipitation of $X$, simply average the index stations:

$$P_x = \frac{1}{M}\left(P_1 + P_2 + \dots + P_M\right)$$

where $M$ is the number of index stations and $P_1 \dots P_M$ are their rainfalls for the same period.

## Method 2 — Normal ratio method

If **any** index station's normal differs from $N_x$ by more than 10 %, weight each station by the ratio of normals:

$$P_x = \frac{N_x}{M}\left(\frac{P_1}{N_1} + \frac{P_2}{N_2} + \dots + \frac{P_M}{N_M}\right)$$

Each term $P_i/N_i$ says "what fraction of its normal did station $i$ get?"; the method assumes $X$ got the same average fraction of **its** normal. This is the method most often required in exams.

> [!exam] The ±10 % test, step by step
> Compute $0.9N_x$ and $1.1N_x$. If all $N_i$ lie inside this band → arithmetic average. If any one lies outside → normal ratio. Write the band explicitly in your answer; the method choice carries marks.

## Method 3 — Regression (correlation) method

When $X$ and one index station $Y$ have a long common record, fit a straight line by least squares to the years when both were observed:

$$P_x = a + b\,P_y, \qquad b = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x \sum y}{n\sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2}, \qquad a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x}$$

(written here with $x = P_y$ and $y = P_x$). Use the line to estimate the missing $P_x$. The **correlation coefficient** $r$ tells you whether the estimate is trustworthy; for rainfall estimation $r$ above about 0.8 is usually expected. With several index stations, **multiple regression** or the normal-ratio method is used instead.

Regression works best for **annual or seasonal** totals. For a single storm or a single day, rainfall at nearby stations is far less correlated, and normal-ratio (or inverse-distance weighting, used in HEC-HMS and GIS) is preferred.

## Worked examples

::widget[missing]

**Slide Q1.** Find the missing storm precipitation of station C.

| Station | A | B | C | D | E |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Storm precipitation (cm) | 9.7 | 8.3 | ? | 11.7 | 8.0 |
| Normal annual precipitation (cm) | 100.3 | 109.5 | 93.5 | 125.7 | 117.5 |

:::sol Show solution
10 % of $N_C$ = 9.35 cm, so the band is 84.15 to 102.85 cm. Only A (100.3) lies inside; B, D and E lie outside → **normal ratio method**.

$P_C = \dfrac{93.5}{4}\left(\dfrac{9.7}{100.3} + \dfrac{8.3}{109.5} + \dfrac{11.7}{125.7} + \dfrac{8.0}{117.5}\right) = 23.375 \times 0.3337 = $ **7.80 cm**.

(The arithmetic average would give 9.43 cm — 21 % higher, because C is normally the driest of the five stations.)
:::


**Example 1 — normal ratio.** Station X did not report for a storm. Three index stations recorded 68, 55 and 82 mm. Normal annual rainfalls: X = 1,010 mm; index stations 1,080, 920 and 1,250 mm. Estimate the storm rainfall at X.

:::sol Show solution
±10 % band of $N_x$: 909 to 1,111 mm. Station 3 (1,250 mm) lies outside, so use the **normal ratio** method.

$P_x = \dfrac{1010}{3}\left(\dfrac{68}{1080} + \dfrac{55}{920} + \dfrac{82}{1250}\right) = 336.7 \times (0.0630 + 0.0598 + 0.0656) = 336.7 \times 0.1883 = $ **63.4 mm**.

(The arithmetic average would have given 68.3 mm — 8 % too high, because the wettest index station is much wetter than X normally is.)
:::

**Example 2 — arithmetic average.** Index stations recorded 52, 61 and 58 mm, with normals 985, 1,042 and 1,068 mm. $N_x$ = 1,015 mm. Estimate $P_x$.

:::sol Show solution
Band: 913.5 to 1,116.5 mm. All three normals lie inside, so $P_x = (52 + 61 + 58)/3 = $ **57.0 mm**.
:::

**Example 3 — regression.** Annual rainfall (mm) at stations Y and X for eight common years:

| Y | 812 | 945 | 701 | 1103 | 880 | 990 | 760 | 1020 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| X | 768 | 902 | 690 | 1049 | 826 | 951 | 712 | 980 |

In a year when X was missing, Y recorded 925 mm. Estimate X.

:::sol Show solution
$n = 8$, $\sum x = 7211$, $\sum y = 6878$. Least squares gives $b = 0.948$, $a = 5.0$, so $P_x = 5.0 + 0.948\,P_y$ with $r = 0.996$.

$P_x = 5.0 + 0.948 \times 925 = $ **882 mm**.

The very high $r$ means the estimate is reliable; the same calculation with $r = 0.5$ would not be worth much.
:::
