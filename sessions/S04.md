> [!note] Key ideas
> - Long precipitation time-series can suffer from **inconsistency** caused by gauge relocation, ecosystem/shrub growth, or changes in instrumentation.
> - The **Double Mass Curve (DMC)** tests consistency by plotting cumulative rainfall at the test station against the cumulative average of surrounding reliable index stations.
> - A distinct change in slope identifies an inconsistency and the year it occurred.
> - Records are adjusted using the slope ratio: $P_{cx} = P_x \times (S_a / S_b)$, where $S_a$ is the slope before the change and $S_b$ is the slope after.

## Why rainfall records need checking

Before any rainfall record is used for design it must be checked for **consistency**: has the station recorded under the same conditions throughout? A record is **inconsistent** when something other than the weather changed the catch partway through. Typical causes:

- The gauge was **relocated** (even a few hundred metres, or to a different exposure).
- The **surroundings changed** — a building or trees grew up near the gauge, or were removed.
- The **instrument** was replaced (Symons' → FRP gauge → tipping bucket), or its height changed.
- The **observation procedure** or observer changed (time of reading, rounding).

A real climatic change affects all stations in the region together; an inconsistency affects only the one station. The **double mass curve** uses exactly this difference.

## The double mass curve technique

**Principle.** The mean accumulated rainfall of a group of surrounding (base) stations is not significantly affected by a change at any single station. So if we plot the **accumulated rainfall of the station under test** against the **accumulated mean rainfall of the base stations**, a consistent record gives a **straight line**. A change in slope (a "break") marks the year in which the test station's recording conditions changed.

### Procedure

1. Choose a group of 5–10 **base stations** in the neighbourhood with consistent records for the same years.
2. Tabulate annual (or seasonal) rainfall of the test station $X$ and the mean of the base stations for each year.
3. Compute the **cumulative** totals for both, year by year. (Subramanya accumulates from the latest year backwards so that the recent, presumably correct, record defines the reference slope; accumulating forwards gives the same break — just be consistent about which period you correct.)
4. Plot cumulative $X$ (y-axis) against cumulative base-station mean (x-axis).
5. Identify any break in slope. Check the station history for a physical cause before accepting it.
6. Compute the slope before the break, $M_a$, and after it, $M_c$, and adjust the inconsistent period.

### The correction

If the period **after** the break is the inconsistent one and the earlier period is taken as correct:

$$P_{cx} = P_x \,\frac{M_a}{M_c}$$

where $P_x$ = recorded rainfall at station $X$ in the inconsistent period, $P_{cx}$ = corrected rainfall, $M_c$ = slope of the double mass curve for the period to be corrected, and $M_a$ = slope for the period taken as correct.

> [!exam] Which period is "correct"?
> The period that matches the station's **current** condition is normally treated as correct, because future data will be collected under it. If the gauge was moved to a better site in 2008, correct the *earlier* record to the new conditions; if it was moved to a worse site, correct the *later* record. In exam problems the question usually tells you; if not, state your assumption.

::widget[dmc]

### Limitations

- A break caused by a **real but local** change in climate (e.g. a large new reservoir) will be "corrected" away wrongly — always look for a physical cause.
- Small random deviations are normal; only a **persistent** change of slope over at least five years should be corrected.
- The base stations themselves must be consistent; test them first against one another.

## Worked example

Annual rainfall (mm) at station M and the mean of four base stations, 2009–2020. The gauge at M was shifted in early 2015. Test the record and correct it to the pre-2015 conditions.

| Year | Base mean | Station M | Σ Base | Σ M |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2009 | 820 | 845 | 820 | 845 |
| 2010 | 955 | 980 | 1775 | 1825 |
| 2011 | 760 | 770 | 2535 | 2595 |
| 2012 | 1010 | 1035 | 3545 | 3630 |
| 2013 | 880 | 905 | 4425 | 4535 |
| 2014 | 915 | 940 | 5340 | 5475 |
| 2015 | 840 | 1015 | 6180 | 6490 |
| 2016 | 990 | 1190 | 7170 | 7680 |
| 2017 | 705 | 850 | 7875 | 8530 |
| 2018 | 1060 | 1270 | 8935 | 9800 |
| 2019 | 930 | 1120 | 9865 | 10920 |
| 2020 | 870 | 1045 | 10735 | 11965 |

:::sol Show solution
Plotting Σ M against Σ Base shows a straight line to 2014 and a steeper line from 2015.

Slope before the break: $M_a = 5475 / 5340 = 1.025$.

Slope after the break: $M_c = (11965 - 5475) / (10735 - 5340) = 6490 / 5395 = 1.203$.

Correction factor $= M_a / M_c = 1.025 / 1.203 = 0.852$.

Corrected values 2015–2020: 1015 × 0.852 = **865**, 1190 → **1014**, 850 → **724**, 1270 → **1082**, 1120 → **955**, 1045 → **891** mm.

Effect: the 12-year mean at M falls from 997 mm (raw) to 917 mm (corrected) — an 8 % difference that would otherwise pass straight into a yield or design calculation.
:::

## Slide example (1937–1972)

Annual rainfall at station X and the average of the nearby stations are given for 1937–1972 (load **Slide data** in the interactive tool above to see the full table). The data are accumulated from the most recent year (1972) backwards, so the recent record defines the reference slope. Check the consistency of X and correct the record.

:::sol Show solution
Plotting cumulative X against cumulative base-station average (from 1972 backwards) gives a straight line up to **1950**, after which (i.e. for 1949 and earlier) the slope increases. The station history should be checked for a change around 1949–50.

Slope 1972–1950: $M_c = \Sigma X / \Sigma B = 5552 / 6486 = 0.856$.

Slope 1949–1937: $M_a = (10048 - 5552)/(10353 - 6486) = 4496 / 3867 = 1.163$.

The recent period represents the present conditions, so the **older** record (1937–1949) is corrected: factor $= 0.856 / 1.163 = 0.736$.

E.g. 1948: $493 \times 0.736 = $ **363 mm**; 1937: $414 \times 0.736 = $ **305 mm**. After correction all points fall on one straight line — check it in the tool above by choosing *treat as correct: first segment*.
:::
### Session 4 recap
1. **Stationarity & consistency:** Hydrologic statistics assume a station's record has not drifted due to external non-climatic influences.
2. **Index group:** Choose 5 to 10 surrounding stations whose records are known to be consistent over the same time horizon.
3. **Slope inflection:** A break in slope indicates a shift in catch efficiency (e.g. tree growth reduces wind speed, increasing catch).
4. **Correction direction:** Historical records are corrected to reflect present-day environmental conditions ($S_a / S_b$).
5. **Quality control in HMS:** HEC-HMS requires consistent, calibrated precipitation records to produce dependable runoff hydrographs.
