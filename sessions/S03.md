> [!note] Key ideas
> - Rainfall depth is measured with **non-recording gauges** (Symons' gauge, read daily at 08:30 IST) and **recording gauges** (float/syphon, tipping-bucket, weighing).
> - Recording gauges record instantaneous intensity and cumulative mass curves continuously.
> - The optimum number of rain-gauge stations is determined by spatial rainfall variability: $N = (C_v / \epsilon)^2$.
> - WMO and IS:4987 specify minimum network density standards for plains, hilly terrain, and arid zones.

## Measuring precipitation

Precipitation is measured as the **vertical depth of water** that would accumulate on a level surface if none of it ran off, infiltrated or evaporated — in millimetres (or centimetres). A **rain gauge** collects the rain falling on a known horizontal opening and measures the collected water.

### Siting a rain gauge (IMD / IS 5225 practice)

- Level, open ground; the gauge in a fenced enclosure of at least 5.5 m × 5.5 m.
- No object (building, tree) closer than about **twice its height** to the gauge, and preferably nothing within 30 m.
- The rim of the collector must be **horizontal** and about **30 cm above ground** — high enough to avoid splash-in, low enough to limit wind error.
- Observations at the same time every day: in India at **08:30 IST**; the reading is credited to the **previous day**.

## Non-recording gauges

### Symons' gauge (the IMD standard non-recording gauge)

A cylindrical gauge with a **127 mm (5 in) diameter** collecting funnel resting on a receiving bottle inside a metal container. Rain collected in the bottle is poured into a **graduated measuring glass** read to 0.1 mm. The receiving bottle normally holds about 75–100 mm of rain; in heavy rain the overflow is caught by the outer container and must be measured too, which means readings may need to be taken more than once a day. IMD's current standard is a fibreglass-reinforced polyester (FRP) gauge of the same principle with a collector area of 100 or 200 cm².

::widget[gauges]

Non-recording gauges give only the **total depth** over the reading interval (usually 24 hours), not when or how intensely it fell.

## Recording (self-recording) gauges

Recording gauges produce a continuous record of cumulative rainfall against time — the **mass curve** — from which intensities for any duration can be read.

### 1. Tipping-bucket gauge
Rain from the funnel falls into one of a pair of small buckets on a pivot. When **0.25 mm** (or 0.2 / 0.5 mm in modern gauges) has collected, the bucket tips, empties, and brings the other bucket under the funnel. Each tip closes an electrical contact and is recorded with its time. Advantages: electronic output, ideal for **telemetry** and automatic weather stations. Disadvantage: under-records in very intense rain (water lost while tipping) and cannot measure snow.

::widget[tipping]

### 2. Weighing-bucket gauge
Rain is caught in a bucket standing on a spring balance or load cell. The weight is recorded continuously against time — directly a mass curve. It can record **snow, hail and rain** together and has a chart that can run for a week.

### 3. Float (natural syphon) gauge — the IMD standard recording gauge
Rain is led into a **float chamber**. As the water rises, a float rises with it and moves a pen on a chart wound on a **clockwork drum**, tracing the mass curve. When the chamber fills (typically after 10 mm of rain) a **syphon** empties it quickly and the pen drops back to zero to start again. The trace therefore looks like a saw-tooth that must be added up. This is the standard self-recording gauge in India and is what the syllabus calls the "syphon type" gauge.

> [!note] Automatic rain gauges (ARGs)
> IMD and state agencies (e.g. KSNDMC in Karnataka) now run large networks of automatic tipping-bucket gauges that report every 15 minutes by telemetry. KSNDMC's network is dense enough to resolve the rainfall of individual Bengaluru wards — useful data for your project.

### Errors in gauge measurement

- Wind: the biggest systematic error — gauges **under-catch**, more so for higher gauges and stronger wind.
- Wetting loss: roughly 0.25 mm needed to wet the funnel and container.
- Evaporation from the receiver before reading; leakage; dents in the collector rim.
- A tilted gauge: about 10° tilt gives roughly 1.5 % less catch.
- Splash-in or splash-out; observer reading errors; wrong date allocation.

## Rain-gauge network

::widget[stormnet]

A gauge samples rain over about 0.0127 m² (a 127 mm funnel) but its reading is used to represent many square kilometres. Rainfall varies in space, so the network must be dense enough to give a reliable catchment average, within limits of cost, access and terrain.

### Recommended densities

**World Meteorological Organization (WMO)** guidance, area per gauge:

| Region | Area per gauge |
| --- | --- |
| Flat regions of temperate, Mediterranean and tropical zones | 600–900 km² (ideal); 900–3,000 km² acceptable |
| Mountainous regions | 100–250 km² (ideal); 250–1,000 km² acceptable in difficult conditions |
| Arid and polar zones | 1,500–10,000 km² depending on feasibility |

**Indian Standard IS 4987 (1994)** recommends:

| Terrain | Area per gauge |
| --- | --- |
| Plains | 520 km² |
| Regions of average elevation ~1,000 m | 260–390 km² |
| Predominantly hilly areas with heavy rainfall | 130 km² |

At least **10 % of the gauges should be self-recording**, so that intensities are known.

### Optimum number of rain gauges

If a catchment has $m$ gauges with rainfall values $P_1, P_2, \dots, P_m$, the coefficient of variation of the rainfall is

$$C_v = \frac{100\,\sigma_{m-1}}{\bar{P}}, \qquad \sigma_{m-1} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (P_i - \bar{P})^2}{m-1}}$$

The optimal number of gauges $N$ needed for an allowable percentage error $\varepsilon$ in the mean rainfall is

$$N = \left(\frac{C_v}{\varepsilon}\right)^2$$

$\varepsilon$ is usually taken as **10 %**. The number of *additional* gauges is $N - m$, rounded up. A smaller allowable error needs many more gauges, since $N$ varies as $1/\varepsilon^2$.

> [!exam] Common mistake
> Use the **sample** standard deviation (divide by $m-1$), as Subramanya does. Using $m$ gives a smaller $C_v$ and a wrong count.

**Example.** A catchment has six gauges. In a normal year they recorded 1,120, 860, 735, 690, 912 and 1,040 mm. How many gauges are needed for a 10 % error in mean rainfall? How many for 15 %?

:::sol Show solution
$\bar{P} = (1120 + 860 + 735 + 690 + 912 + 1040)/6 = 892.8$ mm.

$\sigma_{m-1} = \sqrt{\sum (P_i - \bar{P})^2 / 5} = 167.8$ mm.

$C_v = 100 \times 167.8 / 892.8 = 18.79\,\%$.

For $\varepsilon = 10\,\%$: $N = (18.79/10)^2 = 3.53 \to$ **4 gauges**. The existing six are already sufficient; no additional gauges are needed.

For $\varepsilon = 15\,\%$: $N = (18.79/15)^2 = 1.57 \to 2$ gauges.

The network is adequate for the mean. Whether it is adequate for **storm** rainfall — which varies far more in space than annual totals — is a separate question; repeat the calculation with storm depths and $C_v$ is usually much higher.
:::

::widget[network]

**Slide Q1.** A catchment has five rain-gauge stations. In a year the annual rainfall recorded by them was 89, 54, 45, 41 and 55 cm. If the error in the estimation of rainfall should not exceed 10 %, how many additional gauges are required?

:::sol Show solution
$\bar P = 284/5 = 56.8$ cm; $\sigma_{m-1} = \sqrt{\sum (P_i - \bar P)^2 / 4} = 18.95$ cm; $C_v = 100 \times 18.95 / 56.8 = 33.4\,\%$.

$N = (33.4/10)^2 = 11.1 \to 12$ gauges, so **7 additional gauges** are needed. (Load "Slide Q1" in the calculator above to check.)
:::

**Example.** A 1,800 km² catchment in the plains has 2 gauges. How many are needed by IS 4987, and how many of them should be recording?

:::sol Show solution
Plains: 1 gauge per 520 km² → $1800/520 = 3.46 \to$ **4 gauges**, i.e. 2 more. At least 10 % self-recording → at least **1** recording gauge.
:::
### Session 3 recap
1. **Symons' gauge:** Standard non-recording collector with a 127 mm diameter funnel rim mounted 305 mm above ground to prevent splash.
2. **Recording gauges:** Natural syphon (float) gauges produce a continuous trace on a rotating drum; tipping-bucket gauges record pulses electronically (0.2 mm per tip).
3. **Optimum network:** Gauges are added until the coefficient of variation ($C_v$) of annual rain satisfies the allowable percentage error $\epsilon$.
4. **IS:4987 guidelines:** 1 station per 520 km² in plains; 1 station per 260–390 km² in undulating/hilly regions; 1 station per 1,000 km² in arid zones.
5. **HMS Time-Series Data:** Gages are created in the Time-Series Data Manager before attaching them to meteorologic models.
