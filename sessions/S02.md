## Precipitation

**Precipitation** is all water that reaches the earth's surface from the atmosphere, in liquid or solid form, as a product of condensation of water vapour falling under gravity. It is the main input to every hydrologic analysis: errors in rainfall pass straight into errors in runoff, floods and yield.

### Conditions needed for precipitation

1. The atmosphere must contain **moisture** (the oceans are the chief source).
2. There must be enough **condensation nuclei** (dust, salt, smoke particles) on which droplets form.
3. Weather conditions must cause **cooling** of moist air below its dew point — in practice by **lifting** the air.
4. The products of condensation must grow large enough to **reach the ground** without evaporating on the way.

The last two points explain why most precipitation is classified by the mechanism that lifts the air (below).

## Forms of precipitation

| Form | Description | Typical values |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Rain** | Water drops larger than about 0.5 mm (up to about 6 mm; larger drops break up) | Light: < 2.5 mm/h; moderate: 2.5–7.5 mm/h; heavy: > 7.5 mm/h |
| **Drizzle** | Fine droplets under 0.5 mm, falling slowly | Intensity usually < 1 mm/h |
| **Snow** | Ice crystals, often as flakes | Density 0.06–0.15 g/cm³; in India mostly Himalayan |
| **Glaze (freezing rain)** | Rain or drizzle freezing on contact with ground or objects at or below 0 °C | Ice coating on surfaces |
| **Sleet** | Frozen raindrops — rain that froze while falling through a cold layer | Transparent ice grains |
| **Hail** | Showery pellets or lumps of ice larger than about 8 mm, formed in violent thunderstorms | Occasional in pre-monsoon storms on the Deccan |
| **Dew / frost** | Condensation directly on cool surfaces at night (frost below 0 °C) | Not usually counted as precipitation in budgets |

In Indian hydrology "precipitation" and "rainfall" are often used interchangeably because rain is by far the dominant form outside the Himalaya.

## Types of precipitation (by lifting mechanism)

### 1. Frontal precipitation
When two air masses of different temperature and density meet, the boundary between them is a **front**. The warmer, lighter air is forced up over the colder air, cools, and condenses. A **cold front** (cold air pushing under warm air) gives short, intense rain; a **warm front** (warm air rising gently over cold air) gives long, moderate rain over a wide area. In India, frontal systems matter mainly for the winter rain of the north-west (western disturbances).

### 2. Cyclonic precipitation
A **cyclone** is a large low-pressure region with air spiralling inwards — anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere. Converging air rises and produces rain.

- **Tropical cyclones** (called cyclones in the Indian Ocean, hurricanes in the Atlantic, typhoons in the western Pacific) form over warm seas (sea-surface temperature above about 26–27 °C). The core may be 100–200 km across, with the storm system several hundred kilometres wide. They bring very heavy rain and storm surge to the east coast, e.g. Odisha and Andhra Pradesh.
- **Extratropical cyclones** form along fronts in middle latitudes.

An **anticyclone** is a high-pressure region with outward, clockwise (northern hemisphere) winds of moderate speed. Air subsides, so weather in the centre is calm and dry; some cloud and precipitation may occur at the outer edges.

### 3. Convective precipitation
Local heating of the ground warms the air above it; the warm, light air rises, cools and condenses, often into towering cumulonimbus clouds. Convective storms are **intense, short and local** (areal extent of the order of 10 km). Bengaluru's afternoon and evening thunderstorms in April–May and in the post-monsoon months are classic convective rain — and they are exactly the storms that overload urban drains.

### 4. Orographic precipitation
Moist air blowing against a mountain barrier is forced up its slope, cools, and precipitates on the **windward** side. The **leeward** side receives much less — a **rain-shadow**. The Western Ghats are the textbook case: the south-west monsoon gives more than 3,000–5,000 mm on the windward crest (e.g. Agumbe) while places a short distance east on the plateau receive well under 1,000 mm. The *Arkavathi*, *Cauvery* and *Netravati* basins you may choose for your project sit on either side of this gradient.

## Rainfall in India — the essentials

- **South-west monsoon (June–September)** gives roughly three-quarters of India's annual rainfall. It arrives over Kerala around 1 June and covers the country by mid-July.
- **North-east (retreating) monsoon (October–December)** is the main rainy season for Tamil Nadu, south Andhra Pradesh and parts of south Karnataka.
- **Pre-monsoon (March–May)** thunderstorms and **winter (January–February)** western disturbances add the rest.
- Mean annual rainfall of India is about 1,100–1,200 mm, but it ranges from under 150 mm in western Rajasthan to over 11,000 mm at Mawsynram–Cherrapunji. Bengaluru's mean is about 900–1,000 mm, split between the south-west and north-east monsoons.
- Indian rainfall is concentrated in **a few days** — a large fraction of the annual total falls in the heaviest 10–20 % of rainy days. This concentration is why storage (reservoirs, tanks, aquifers) is central to Indian water management.

## How precipitation enters a hydrologic model

A rainfall-runoff model needs to know, for each part of the catchment:

1. **How much** rain fell (depth), and
2. **When** it fell (its time distribution, or hyetograph).

Point measurements from gauges give both at a few places. The **meteorologic model** of HEC-HMS is the component that turns gauge data (or a design storm) into rainfall on each subbasin. Sessions 3 to 8 build that component step by step: gauges (S03), data checks (S04), areal averaging (S05), gap filling (S06), presentation (S07) and design storms (S08).
