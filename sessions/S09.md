## From paper maps to GIS

Sessions 5 and 6 drew Thiessen polygons and catchments by hand. Today we do the same with a **Geographic Information System** (GIS). The theory is unchanged; GIS just does the geometry precisely and repeatably, and lets us handle real station networks and real terrain. This session also launches your **PBL project**, which uses these tools from now on.

## Thiessen polygons in GIS (Voronoi polygons)

In computational geometry the Thiessen polygons of a set of points are called **Voronoi polygons** (or a Voronoi tessellation). The GIS procedure mirrors the hand method:

1. Load the gauge locations as a **point layer** (from a table of latitude/longitude).
2. **Project** the data to a metric coordinate system so that areas come out in m² — for Karnataka, **UTM zone 43N (EPSG:32643)**. Never compute areas in degrees.
3. Generate **Voronoi polygons** around the points, with a buffer large enough to cover the catchment.
4. **Clip** the polygons to the catchment boundary.
5. Compute each clipped polygon's **area**; weight = area ÷ catchment area.

A gauge outside the catchment still generates a polygon, and if that polygon overlaps the catchment the gauge gets a weight — exactly as TG4 did in Session 5.

## Delineating a catchment from a DEM

A **Digital Elevation Model** (DEM) is a grid of ground elevations. Freely available DEMs for India include **CartoDEM** (30 m, from ISRO's Bhuvan portal, W5) and **SRTM** (30 m). From a DEM a GIS (or HEC-HMS itself) delineates catchments in a fixed sequence:

| Step | What it does | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fill sinks** | Raises isolated low cells (pits) to the level of their lowest neighbour | Pits are usually DEM errors; water would get "stuck" there |
| **Flow direction** | For each cell, the neighbour of steepest descent (the D8 method: one of 8 directions) | Defines where water from each cell goes |
| **Flow accumulation** | Number (or area) of upstream cells draining through each cell | High values trace the stream network |
| **Stream definition** | Cells with accumulation above a **threshold area** are streams | The threshold controls how detailed the network is |
| **Outlet (pour point)** | The point at which the catchment is defined — snapped onto the stream | A catchment exists only relative to an outlet |
| **Watershed** | All cells draining to the outlet | Gives the catchment boundary and area |

::widget[d8]

> [!warn] Flat cities fool DEMs
> In flat urban areas, drains, culverts, road embankments and lake bunds control where water goes, and a 30 m DEM cannot see them. Delineations in places like Bengaluru must be checked against drainage maps and site visits. Your PBL report should say how you checked yours.

## Catchment characteristics

Once a catchment is delineated, GIS gives the parameters that control its runoff response (used again in Sessions 16, 20 and 27):

- **Area** $A$ — the single most important parameter; peak flow and volume scale with it.
- **Length** $L$ — length of the main stream (or of the basin) from outlet to divide.
- **Form factor** $F = A/L^2$. Small $F$ → long, narrow catchment → lower, later peak.
- **Compactness coefficient** (Gravelius) $C_c = \dfrac{P}{2\sqrt{\pi A}}$, the ratio of the perimeter $P$ to the circumference of a circle of the same area. $C_c = 1$ for a circle; larger values → more elongated.
- **Drainage density** $D_d = \dfrac{\sum L_s}{A}$ — total stream length per unit area. High $D_d$ → quick response.
- **Slope** of the catchment and of the main stream — steeper → faster response.

**Example.** A catchment has area 320 km², perimeter 96 km and main-stream length 34 km. Find $F$ and $C_c$.

:::sol Show solution
$F = 320/34^2 = 320/1156 = $ **0.28**.

$C_c = 96 / (2\sqrt{\pi \times 320}) = 96/63.4 = $ **1.51**.

An elongated catchment: expect a flatter, later flood peak than a compact catchment of the same area.
:::
