## What hydrology is

**Hydrology** is the science that deals with the occurrence, circulation and distribution of water on the earth and in its atmosphere, and with its physical and chemical properties and its interaction with the environment. Subramanya (T1) puts it compactly: the study of water in all its forms, from its origins to all its destinations on the earth.

**Engineering (applied) hydrology** is the part of that science an engineer uses to plan, design and operate works that store, convey or control water. It answers three practical kinds of question:

1. **How much water is there?** Estimating the water resources of a basin — the yield of a catchment, the safe draft from an aquifer, the storage needed in a reservoir or a rooftop tank.
2. **How do the processes behave?** Precipitation, evaporation, transpiration, infiltration and runoff, and how they interact.
3. **What are the extremes?** Floods (design flood for a spillway, culvert, storm drain or bridge) and droughts (minimum flows, storage for a dry year).

Hydrology is usually split into **surface-water hydrology** (water above the ground surface: rain, streams, lakes, reservoirs) and **groundwater hydrology** (water below the ground surface: soil moisture, aquifers, wells). Units I–IV of this course are mostly surface water; Unit V is groundwater.

### Why it matters — especially here

- The total quantity of water on the planet is effectively fixed; demand is not. Socio-economic development of a region tracks the water available to it.
- India has about 18 % of the world's population and about 4 % of its renewable freshwater, delivered mostly in four monsoon months. Storage, groundwater and harvesting exist because of that mismatch in time.
- Bengaluru shows both extremes in one city: urban floods after short intense storms (2017, 2022) and falling groundwater levels between them. Both are hydrology problems before they are drainage or supply problems.

> [!exam] Exam tip
> A 5-mark "importance of hydrology" answer should name applications (water supply, irrigation, hydropower, flood control, navigation, drainage, pollution control) **and** give at least one design quantity hydrology supplies, e.g. "the design flood for a spillway" or "the dependable yield of a catchment".

## The hydrologic cycle

The **hydrologic cycle** is the continuous circulation of water between the oceans, the atmosphere and the land, driven by solar energy and gravity. Water evaporates from oceans and land, is carried as vapour, condenses and falls as precipitation. Part of the precipitation is intercepted by vegetation, part is held in depressions, part infiltrates the soil, and the rest flows over land into streams. Infiltrated water feeds soil moisture, moves laterally as interflow or percolates to groundwater, which returns slowly to streams as base flow. Evaporation and transpiration return water to the atmosphere at every stage.

Three statements worth memorising:

- The cycle has **no beginning or end**; it is convenient to start at evaporation from the oceans.
- Globally it is a **closed system** (the total water is constant); for a catchment it is an **open system** (water enters and leaves across its boundaries).
- Water is stored in many places for very different **residence times** — days in the atmosphere, weeks in rivers, years to millennia in deep aquifers and ice.

## The catchment

The **catchment** (also *drainage basin*, *watershed* or *drainage area*) of a stream at a given point is the area of land whose runoff drains to that point. It is bounded by a **divide** (ridge line) traced on a topographic map or computed from a digital elevation model (DEM). We normally assume the groundwater divide coincides with the surface divide; where it does not (karst, deep aquifers) the water budget will not close.

## The water-budget equation

The hydrologic cycle obeys conservation of mass. For any control volume — a lake, a catchment, a city — over a time interval $\Delta t$:

$$\text{Mass inflow} - \text{Mass outflow} = \text{Change in mass storage}$$

Because the density of water is constant, this is written in volumes:

$$V_i - V_o = \Delta S$$

For a catchment over interval $\Delta t$ the **water-budget equation** is

$$P - R - G - E - T = \Delta S$$

where $P$ = precipitation, $R$ = surface runoff leaving the catchment, $G$ = net groundwater flow out of the catchment, $E$ = evaporation, $T$ = transpiration, and $\Delta S$ = change in storage. All terms are usually expressed as **depth over the catchment** (mm or cm), i.e. volume ÷ catchment area.

The storage $S$ has three parts: surface water storage $S_s$ (lakes, ponds, channels), soil moisture $S_m$, and groundwater $S_g$:

$$\Delta S = \Delta S_s + \Delta S_m + \Delta S_g$$

### The rainfall–runoff form

Grouping all the losses (evaporation, transpiration, infiltration to deep groundwater, storage changes) into $L$:

$$R = P - L$$

and the ratio $R/P$ is the **runoff coefficient**. Over a long period (say a year, from one dry season to the next) $\Delta S \approx 0$, so annual runoff + annual evapotranspiration ≈ annual rainfall. This is the simplest and most-used result in the whole course.

> [!note] Units you will use all semester
> 1 mm of rain on 1 km² = 1,000 m³. 1 m³/s flowing for one day = 86,400 m³. 1 hectare = 10⁴ m². Convert everything to depth over the catchment **or** everything to volume before adding.

## Worked examples

**Example 1 — lake budget.** A lake has a surface area of 145 ha. In a 30-day month the average inflow is 6.0 m³/s and the average outflow 6.5 m³/s. Rainfall on the lake was 14.5 cm, lake evaporation 6.1 cm and seepage loss 1.8 cm. Find the change in lake level.

:::sol Show solution
Convert flows to depth over the lake. Net inflow volume $= (6.0 - 6.5) \times 30 \times 86400 = -1.296 \times 10^6$ m³.

Depth over the lake $= -1.296 \times 10^6 / 1.45 \times 10^6 = -0.894$ m.

$\Delta S = -0.894 + 0.145 - 0.061 - 0.018 = -0.828$ m.

The lake level **falls by about 0.83 m** (a storage loss of about 1.20 × 10⁶ m³). Notice that the river flows dominate; rainfall and evaporation are corrections of a few centimetres.
:::

**Example 2 — runoff coefficient of a storm.** A 220 ha catchment receives 9.5 cm of rain in 2 hours. The stream at the outlet was dry before the storm, then carried an average of 2.1 m³/s for 8 hours, and was dry again. Find (a) the water not available as runoff and (b) the runoff coefficient.

:::sol Show solution
Rainfall volume $= 0.095 \times 220 \times 10^4 = 209{,}000$ m³.

Runoff volume $= 2.1 \times 8 \times 3600 = 60{,}480$ m³.

(a) Losses (infiltration + evaporation + transpiration + depression storage) $= 209{,}000 - 60{,}480 = 148{,}520$ m³ ≈ **1.49 × 10⁵ m³**.

(b) Runoff coefficient $= 60{,}480 / 209{,}000 = $ **0.29**.
:::

**Example 3 — annual evapotranspiration.** A river basin of 5,200 km² has a mean annual flow of 68 m³/s and a mean annual rainfall of 1.15 m. Estimate the annual evapotranspiration, assuming no net change in storage or groundwater export over a year.

:::sol Show solution
Annual runoff volume $= 68 \times 365 \times 86400 = 2.144 \times 10^9$ m³.

Runoff depth $= 2.144 \times 10^9 / (5200 \times 10^6) = 0.412$ m.

$ET = P - R = 1.15 - 0.41 = $ **0.74 m/year** (about 64 % of the rainfall). That is a typical figure for a peninsular Indian basin.
:::
