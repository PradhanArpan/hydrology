## What hydrology is

*Hydro* (water) + *logy* (study): **hydrology** is the science of water. It deals with the **occurrence, circulation, distribution and movement** of water on the earth's surface, below it and in the atmosphere. Subramanya (T1) defines it as *the study of water in all its forms, from its origins to all its destinations on the earth*.

Hydrology is divided into **scientific hydrology** (understanding the processes) and **applied or engineering hydrology** (using that understanding to plan, design and operate water works). Engineering hydrology answers three kinds of question:

1. **Estimation of water resources** — how much water is there in a basin, a reservoir, an aquifer?
2. **Study of processes** — precipitation, evapotranspiration, infiltration and runoff, and how they interact.
3. **Study of problems** — floods and droughts, and strategies to combat them.

![Root-zone water budget for irrigation: precipitation and irrigation in; ET, surface runoff and deep percolation out — Slide: CE533 Unit I](assets/img/ppt/root_zone_budget.png)

Based on where the water is, hydrology is split into:

- **Surface-water hydrology** — the occurrence and distribution of water *above* the earth's surface: oceans, lakes, ponds, rivers, streams.
- **Groundwater hydrology** — the occurrence and distribution of water *below* the surface: soil moisture, the unsaturated zone, aquifers.

### The civil engineer as hydrologist

| Field | What hydrology supplies |
| --- | --- |
| Reservoirs and dams | Rainfall analysis, runoff and streamflow, available water, **reservoir capacity**, the **maximum probable flood** for the spillway |
| Irrigation and agriculture | Precipitation, soil moisture, evapotranspiration — crop water demand |
| Drought monitoring | Precipitation, soil moisture, ET, runoff, streamflow and groundwater together |
| Drainage, culverts, bridges | **Expected maximum flows** at the structure (the design flood) |
| Water supply and sewerage | Dependable yield of sources; storm-water quantities |
| River works | Effect of dams on the river downstream |

> [!exam] Exam tip
> A 5-mark "importance of hydrology" answer names the applications above **and** the design quantity each one needs — e.g. "the spillway is designed for the maximum probable flood estimated by hydrological analysis".

### River basins

India is divided into about 20 river basins (CWC classification), from the Ganga (about 8.6 lakh km²) and Indus to the small west-flowing rivers of the Western Ghats. The **average annual water resource potential** of all basins is about **1,869 km³** (billion m³), of which only about **690 km³** of surface water is **utilisable** because of topography and the seasonality of flow. ### Challenges — the Indian scenario

1. **Growing population and demand.** Per capita availability fell to about **1,545 m³/year** (2011) — below the water-stress threshold of 1,700 m³/year. Demand is expected to rise by about 38 % in four decades. Irrigation is the largest user, and water-use efficiency is only about 35–40 %.
2. **Pollution.** Partially treated or untreated municipal sewage is about 75 % of the pollution load on rivers; sewage treatment capacity covered only about a third of the sewage generated (CPCB, 2015). Groundwater is contaminated by arsenic, fluoride and iron in many districts.
3. **Seasonal and spatial extremes.** Rain falls in 4–5 months; about one-sixth of the country (≈ 40 Mha) is drought-prone, while about 7.5 Mha is flooded in a typical year.
4. **Trans-boundary issues.** The Ganga–Brahmaputra–Barak system is international; most other basins are inter-state. Water is a **State subject** with many rights holders.
5. **Fragmented management.** Many institutions (CWC, CPCB, CGWB, state departments) manage parts of the same river, often town by town rather than basin by basin. Upstream actions affect downstream users regardless of administrative boundaries — which is why **basin-scale** (catchment) thinking runs through this whole course.

## The hydrologic cycle

The **hydrologic cycle** is the continuous circulation of water between the oceans, the atmosphere and the land, driven by solar energy and gravity. Water evaporates, is carried as vapour, condenses and falls as precipitation; part is intercepted, part stored in depressions, part infiltrates, and the rest flows over land to streams. Infiltrated water becomes soil moisture, interflow or groundwater, which returns slowly to streams as base flow. Evaporation and transpiration return water to the atmosphere at every stage.

![The hydrologic cycle — Slide (after USGS)](assets/img/ppt/cycle_usgs.jpg)

Remember:

- The cycle has **no beginning or end**.
- **Oceans** are the main reservoir and source; the **atmosphere** carries and delivers water; the **land** uses it.
- Globally the cycle is a **closed system**; for a catchment it is an **open system** — water enters and leaves across its boundary, so the water available at a place changes with time.

## The catchment

The **catchment** (also *catchment area, drainage area, drainage basin, watershed*) of a stream at a point is the area of land that drains to that point. It is separated from neighbouring catchments by a ridge called the **divide**. Its extent is found by tracing the ridge on a topographic map (or computing it from a DEM) and measuring the area.

We normally assume the **groundwater (phreatic) divide** coincides with the **topographic divide**. Where an impervious layer slopes across the divide they differ, and groundwater can leak into or out of the catchment — the water budget will then not close.

## The water-budget equation

Conservation of mass for any control volume over a time interval $\Delta t$:

$$\text{Mass inflow} - \text{Mass outflow} = \text{Change in mass storage} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad V_i - V_o = \Delta S$$

For a catchment:

$$P - R - G - E - T = \Delta S$$

$P$ = precipitation, $R$ = surface runoff, $G$ = net groundwater flow out, $E$ = evaporation, $T$ = transpiration, $\Delta S$ = change in storage. All terms are usually written as **depth over the catchment** (volume ÷ area). The storage has three parts:

$$\Delta S = \Delta S_s + \Delta S_m + \Delta S_g$$

(surface water, soil moisture, groundwater). Lumping all losses into $L$ gives the **rainfall–runoff relationship** $R = P - L$; the ratio $R/P$ is the **runoff coefficient**. Over a full year $\Delta S \approx 0$, so annual runoff + annual evapotranspiration ≈ annual rainfall.

> [!note] Units you will use all semester
> 1 mm on 1 km² = 1,000 m³ · 1 m³/s for a day = 86,400 m³ · 1 ha = 10⁴ m² · 1 m³/s for a year = 31.54 × 10⁶ m³. Convert everything to depth **or** everything to volume before adding.

## Problems from the lecture slides

**Slide Q1 — lake budget.** A lake had a water-surface elevation of 103.200 m above datum at the beginning of a month. In that month it received an average inflow of 6 m³/s from surface runoff, and the average outflow was 6.5 m³/s. It received 145 mm of rainfall and lake evaporation was 6.10 cm. The average lake surface area is 5,000 ha; there is no groundwater contribution. Write the water budget and find the elevation at the end of the month.

:::sol Show solution
Take a 30-day month. Net river inflow volume $= (6.0 - 6.5) \times 30 \times 86400 = -1.296 \times 10^6$ m³; as depth over $5000 \times 10^4$ m²: $-0.0259$ m.

$\Delta S = I - Q + P - E = -0.0259 + 0.145 - 0.061 = +0.058$ m.

Elevation at month end $= 103.200 + 0.058 = $ **103.258 m**. (A 31-day month gives 103.257 m.)
:::

**Slide Q2 — storm losses.** A 150 ha catchment received 10.5 cm of rain in 90 minutes. The stream at the outlet was dry before the storm, then carried an average of 1.5 m³/s for 10 hours, and was dry again. (a) How much water was not available to runoff because of infiltration, evaporation and transpiration? (b) What is the runoff/rainfall ratio?

:::sol Show solution
Rain volume $= 0.105 \times 150 \times 10^4 = 157{,}500$ m³. Runoff volume $= 1.5 \times 10 \times 3600 = 54{,}000$ m³.

(a) Losses $= 157{,}500 - 54{,}000 = $ **103,500 m³**. (b) Runoff coefficient $= 54{,}000/157{,}500 = $ **0.343**.
:::

**Slide Q3 — Gandak basin.** The drainage area of the river Gandak is 11,839 km². The mean annual runoff is 144.4 m³/s and the average annual rainfall is 1.08 m. Estimate the annual ET loss and compare it with the lake evaporation of 1 m/year measured at Muzaffarpur.

:::sol Show solution
Annual runoff volume $= 144.4 \times 365 \times 86400 = 4.554 \times 10^9$ m³ → depth $= 4.554 \times 10^9 / 11839 \times 10^6 = 0.385$ m.

$ET = P - R = 1.08 - 0.385 = $ **0.695 m/year**.

This is lower than the 1 m/year lake evaporation because a lake evaporates at the potential rate all year, while the land surface runs short of water in the dry months — actual ET is limited by available soil moisture (Session 14).
:::

**Example — runoff coefficient.** A 220 ha catchment gets 9.5 cm of rain in 2 h; the outlet then carries 2.1 m³/s for 8 h. Find the losses and the runoff coefficient. *(Answer: 148,520 m³; 0.29. Try it in the calculator above with the "Example 2" button.)*
