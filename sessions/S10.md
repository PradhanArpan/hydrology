> [!note] Key ideas
> - Not all rain becomes runoff. The parts held back are the **abstractions** (or losses): **interception**, **depression storage**, **evaporation**, **transpiration** and **infiltration**.
> - For a single flood-producing storm, **infiltration** is by far the largest loss; over a year, **evapotranspiration** is.
> - **Evaporation** is a net flux of water molecules from a liquid surface to the air. It continues only while the vapour pressure at the water surface $e_w$ exceeds that of the air $e_a$.
> - **Dalton's law:** $E_L = C\,(e_w - e_a)$. Evaporation also rises with temperature and wind, and falls with dissolved salts and higher air pressure.

## Where does the rain go?

Watch a storm on a forested hillside. The first few millimetres never reach the ground; they wet the leaves and stay there (**interception**). Rain that reaches the ground soaks into the soil (**infiltration**). When rain falls faster than the soil can take it, water collects in hollows and puddles (**depression storage**). Only after these are satisfied does water move over the surface to a stream as **runoff**. Between storms, the water held on leaves, in puddles and in the soil returns to the air by **evaporation** and **transpiration**.

Hydrologists call everything that does not become direct runoff an **abstraction** or a **loss** — "loss" from the point of view of the stream, not of the catchment.

$$\text{Direct runoff} = P - (\text{interception} + \text{depression storage} + \text{infiltration} + \text{evaporation during the storm})$$

| Abstraction | What happens | Typical size | Matters most for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Interception** | Rain caught on leaves, stems and roofs, later evaporated | 0.25–1.25 mm storage per storm; 10–20 % of annual rain in dense forest | Light, frequent showers; forested catchments |
| **Depression storage** | Water held in surface hollows, ditches, puddles | about 2.5 mm (clay) to 5 mm (sand) | Flat land, the start of a storm |
| **Infiltration** | Water entering the soil surface | from < 1 mm/h (clay, paved) to > 25 mm/h (sand) | Every storm — the largest event loss |
| **Evaporation** | Liquid water → vapour from water and soil surfaces | 2–8 mm/day in India | Water balance of lakes and reservoirs |
| **Transpiration** | Water drawn up by roots and released through leaves | similar order to evaporation | Seasonal and annual water balance |

> [!exam] Event scale vs annual scale
> During a short, intense storm, evaporation and transpiration are tiny (a few tenths of a mm per hour) and are usually ignored. Over a year they dominate. Of the roughly 4,000 km³ of precipitation India receives in a year, the average annual flow in its rivers is only about 1,869 km³ (Central Water Commission estimate) — most of the rest goes back to the atmosphere.

## Interception

Interception is the part of rainfall caught by vegetation (and, in cities, by roofs and other surfaces) that evaporates without reaching the ground. Part of the caught water drips off (**throughfall**) or runs down the trunk (**stemflow**); only what stays and evaporates is the interception loss.

Horton (1919) expressed the interception loss in one storm as

$$I_i = S_i + K_i\,E\,t$$

where $I_i$ = interception loss (mm), $S_i$ = interception storage that the leaves can hold (0.25–1.25 mm), $K_i$ = ratio of the vegetal surface area to its projected area on the ground, $E$ = evaporation rate during the storm (mm/h), and $t$ = duration of the storm (h).

It depends on the **type and density of vegetation**, the **season** (leaf cover), and the **storm**: a light 3 mm shower may be lost almost completely, while in a 100 mm storm interception is a few per cent.

## Depression storage

Water collected in surface depressions once the rainfall rate exceeds the infiltration rate. It is later lost to evaporation or infiltration — it never reaches the stream directly. The storage depends on the **surface roughness**, **slope** and **land use**; typical maximum depths are about **5 mm for sand, 4 mm for loam and 2.5 mm for clay**. Depressions fill gradually, and Linsley et al. give the volume in storage as

$$V_s = S_d\left(1 - e^{-k P_e}\right)$$

where $S_d$ = depression storage capacity (mm), $P_e$ = rainfall in excess of infiltration (mm), and $k$ = a constant of the surface.

> [!hms] In HEC-HMS
> Each abstraction has its own slot in the subbasin: **Canopy** (interception), **Surface** (depression storage), **Loss** (infiltration) and, for continuous runs, the meteorologic model's **Evapotranspiration**. You will find each of them in today's lab.

## Evaporation: the process

Evaporation turns liquid water into vapour. Water molecules at the surface constantly escape into the air and others return. **Net evaporation** happens only when more leave than return, which depends on two things:

1. **Energy.** Each kilogram evaporated takes about 2.45 MJ of heat (latent heat of vaporisation, ≈ 585 cal/g at 20 °C). The sun supplies most of it, which is why evaporation peaks in April–May in peninsular India.
2. **A vapour-pressure difference.** The thin layer of air in contact with the water is saturated at the water's temperature, with vapour pressure $e_w$. The air above has vapour pressure $e_a$. Vapour diffuses from high to low: evaporation continues while $e_w > e_a$, and the vapour must be carried away (wind, turbulence) for it to go on.

### Saturation vapour pressure

The most vapour that air can hold rises steeply with temperature:

| Temperature (°C) | 0 | 5 | 10 | 15 | 20 | 25 | 30 | 35 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $e_s$ (mm Hg) | 4.58 | 6.54 | 9.21 | 12.79 | 17.54 | 23.76 | 31.82 | 42.18 |

The actual vapour pressure of air is $e_a = \text{RH} \times e_s(T_{air})$, where RH is the relative humidity as a fraction.

### Dalton's law

Dalton (1802) stated that the rate of evaporation is proportional to the difference in vapour pressures:

$$E_L = C\,(e_w - e_a)$$

where $E_L$ = rate of evaporation (mm/day), $e_w$ = saturation vapour pressure at the **water-surface** temperature (mm Hg), $e_a$ = actual vapour pressure of the **air** (mm Hg), and $C$ = a coefficient that includes the effect of wind and the site.

Nearly every empirical evaporation formula — including Meyer's formula in Session 11 — is Dalton's law with a wind term built into $C$.

> [!warn] Water temperature, not air temperature
> $e_w$ is read at the temperature of the **water surface**; $e_a$ uses the **air** temperature and humidity. Swapping them is a common exam mistake. If $e_a > e_w$, the answer is negative: there is condensation, not evaporation.

## Factors affecting evaporation

| Factor | Effect on evaporation | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vapour-pressure deficit** $(e_w - e_a)$ | Increases | The driving force in Dalton's law |
| **Temperature** (water and air) | Increases | Higher $e_w$; more energy available. The *rate* does not follow air temperature exactly, because the water's temperature lags behind it |
| **Wind** | Increases, up to a limit | Removes the saturated layer above the water; above a critical speed, extra wind has little effect |
| **Atmospheric pressure** | Decreases as pressure rises | Denser air slows the escape of molecules; evaporation is higher at high altitudes, all else equal |
| **Dissolved salts** | Decreases | About 1 % less evaporation for every 1 % increase in specific gravity; sea water evaporates 2–3 % less than fresh water |
| **Heat storage in the water body** | Shifts the season | Deep lakes store heat: they evaporate less than shallow ones in summer and more in winter. Shallow ponds follow the weather closely |
| **Surface area and exposure** | Increases with area exposed to sun and wind | A wide, shallow reservoir loses more water per unit storage than a deep one |

## Worked examples

### Example 1 — Dalton's law

A lake has a water-surface temperature of 25 °C. The air above is at 30 °C with a relative humidity of 50 %. For this lake $C = 0.40$ mm/day per mm Hg. Estimate the daily evaporation.

:::sol Show solution
From the table: $e_w = e_s(25\,°\text{C}) = 23.76$ mm Hg; $e_s(30\,°\text{C}) = 31.82$ mm Hg.

$e_a = 0.50 \times 31.82 = 15.91$ mm Hg.

$E_L = 0.40 \times (23.76 - 15.91) = 0.40 \times 7.85 = \mathbf{3.14}$ mm/day.
:::

### Example 2 — When the answer is negative

On a humid monsoon night, a pond is at 20 °C and the air is at 25 °C with RH = 80 %. Will the pond evaporate?

:::sol Show solution
$e_w = 17.54$ mm Hg; $e_a = 0.80 \times 23.76 = 19.01$ mm Hg.

$e_w - e_a = -1.47$ mm Hg $< 0$. The air holds more vapour than the water surface can, so the net flux is **towards** the water: there is condensation (dew), not evaporation.
:::

### Example 3 — Interception in a forest storm

A 6-hour storm of 40 mm falls on a forest with $S_i = 1.0$ mm and $K_i = 3$. The evaporation rate during the storm is 0.1 mm/h. Find the interception loss.

:::sol Show solution
$I_i = S_i + K_i E t = 1.0 + 3 \times 0.1 \times 6 = \mathbf{2.8}$ mm.

That is $2.8 / 40 = 7\%$ of the storm. For a 4 mm shower the same forest would intercept nearly all of it.
:::

### Example 4 — Depression storage

A loam surface has $S_d = 4$ mm and $k = 0.2$ per mm. Rainfall in excess of infiltration so far is 10 mm. How much water is in depression storage?

:::sol Show solution
$V_s = 4\left(1 - e^{-0.2 \times 10}\right) = 4\,(1 - 0.1353) = \mathbf{3.46}$ mm.

The depressions are 86 % full; most of any further excess will run off.
:::

### Session 10 recap

1. **Abstractions** = interception + depression storage + infiltration + evaporation + transpiration. Runoff is what is left.
2. **Event vs year:** infiltration dominates a storm; evapotranspiration dominates the annual budget.
3. **Interception** (Horton): $I_i = S_i + K_i E t$. **Depression storage** (Linsley): $V_s = S_d(1 - e^{-kP_e})$.
4. **Dalton's law:** $E_L = C(e_w - e_a)$ — $e_w$ at the water temperature, $e_a$ from the air temperature and humidity.
5. **Factors:** vapour-pressure deficit, temperature, wind (up to a limit), pressure, dissolved salts, and heat storage in the water body.
